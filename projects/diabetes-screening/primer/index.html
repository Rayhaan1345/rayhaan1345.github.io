What are we predicting?

Guess first. A model answers "no diabetes" for all 768 women in the data, without looking at a single measurement. Could it still be right most of the time?

model
a recipe that turns measurements into a score
label
the known answer for a woman in the data, diabetes or not
base rate
the share of women whose label is diabetes

A useful model has to beat the lazy answer, and here the lazy answer is right 65% of the time.

A third of the women have diabetes, so saying "no" to all of them is right for the other two thirds.

The data comes from a study of 768 women aged 21 or older. Glucose, BMI, blood pressure and five other things were measured, and the study recorded who had diabetes. Of the 768, 268 had diabetes, a base rate of 34.9%.

268women with diabetes÷768all women=34.9%base rate

Switch to "always say no". The lazy model calls all 500 women without diabetes correctly:

500women without diabetes÷768all women=65.1%lazy model's accuracy

It also catches none of the 268 women with diabetes. A grade of 65% sounds fine and hides a useless screening test.

Common belief. A model that is right two times in three has learned something.

Why it fails. The lazy model reaches 65% without reading any measurement.

Better account. Compare a model with the base rate, and count missed cases on their own.

Quick quiz

What is the base rate here, and why does it matter?

It is 34.9%. Any model has to beat the 65.1% that a lazy "no" gets.

Why is accuracy alone a poor grade for a screening test?

A model can score well while missing most women with diabetes, as the lazy model shows.

Grading on women the model never saw

Guess first. You prepare for an exam with a practice paper that comes with its answers. You score 100% on the practice paper. What does that tell you about your exam score?

training set
the women the model learns from
held-out set
women kept aside and used only to grade the model
accuracy
the share of women the model calls correctly

You grade a model on women it never saw while learning, because its score on the women it learned from is too kind.

The split sets aside 154 women at random, and both groups keep about the same share with diabetes.

Before any learning, the project set aside 154 of the 768 women. The model learns from the other 614 and is graded on the 154. Choosing the held-out women at random keeps the grade fair, because the two groups look alike.

accuracy=correct callscaught + cleared÷women graded614 in training, 154 held out

Meet Woman A, one of the 154 held-out women. Her glucose is 158 mg/dL, her BMI 31.6 and her age 28, and she had diabetes. The model never sees her label while it learns. You will follow her through the rest of the page.

Analogy. The training set is the practice paper with answers. The held-out set is the exam.

Where it breaks. An exam asks new questions on the same course. The held-out women come from the same study, so a good grade says little about women from a different population. The last section returns to this.

Quick quiz

Why can't you grade the model on its training women?

It has seen their answers, so its score there is too kind and says little about new women.

When do you choose the held-out set?

Before any learning or tuning. If you use it to pick settings, it stops being a fair test.

One rule

Guess first. You may ask one question about one measurement. Which measurement would you pick, and where would you draw the line?

cut-off
the value above which the rule calls diabetes
caught, missed
women with diabetes the rule calls yes, or calls no
false alarm, cleared
women without diabetes the rule calls yes, or calls no

A single cut-off on glucose is already a model, and it gets about three in four held-out women right.

Women with diabetes tend to have higher glucose, so one line already sorts most of them.

Drag the line or use the slider. The shaded side is called diabetes, and the four counts are the four outcomes of any yes-or-no test. Accuracy counts the two kinds of correct call:

training accuracy=(68caught+388cleared)÷614training women=74.3%moves with the line

Training this rule is a search. The computer tries all cut-offs from 60 to 199 and keeps the one with the highest training accuracy. That is 144 mg/dL, and it is right for 75.3% of the held-out women.

Try this. Move the line left until the rule catches most women with diabetes. Watch the false alarms.

Where it breaks. One rule reads one measurement. Two women with the same glucose get the same call, whatever their BMI or age.

The trade-off. Moving the line left catches more cases and raises more false alarms. For screening, a missed case usually costs more than a false alarm, which argues for a lower cut-off than the most accurate one.

Quick quiz

Name the four outcomes of a yes-or-no test.

Caught, missed, false alarm and cleared.

Why might a screening test use a lower cut-off than the most accurate one?

A missed case usually costs more than a false alarm, which a follow-up test can sort out.

Decision trees

Guess first. A woman's glucose is high. Which second question would help you decide?

decision tree
questions in a chain, where the next question depends on the last answer
leaf
the end of a path, where the tree makes its call
depth
how many questions the tree asks in a row
training
searching for the questions that sort the training women best
Gini impurity
how mixed a box is: 0 when all its women share one label, 0.5 when the labels split half and half

A tree chains questions, and training it means searching for the questions that sort the training women best.

A tree grows by cutting one box in two at a time, and a box turns coral when most of its training women had diabetes.

Start with one box of all 614 training women. Choose glucose or BMI, drag the line to a cut-off, and press "Split here". Click a box, on the plot or in the tree, to choose the next box to cut. Coral diamonds had diabetes, navy circles did not, and the star is Woman A. The plot draws the training women. "Show held-out" adds the 154 held-out women as open symbols, and the readouts under the figure give the accuracy on both groups.

Now press "Let the computer choose" to run the search that training performs. It tries all cut-offs of glucose and of BMI in the chosen box. It scores them by the Gini impurity of the two new boxes, weighted by their size, and moves the line to the lowest score. For the first box it picks glucose at most 127.5, which lowers the weighted Gini from 0.453 to 0.373. The small charts plot the scores of all cut-offs.

Try this. Cut the plot into four boxes by hand, then compare your weighted Gini with the computer's best for four boxes in the last readout. The search looks one cut ahead, so a patient reader sometimes wins.

Where does Woman A go? Her glucose of 158 and BMI of 31.6 put her in one box, and the share of training women with diabetes in that box decides her call. With no cut yet her box is the whole group, so the share is 35% and the call is no. Cut the plot and watch her box change. Suppose the computer cuts the whole group at glucose 127.5, then the high-glucose box at BMI 29.95. Her box then has 163 women, and 118 of them, 72%, had diabetes:

share in her boxno question yet, so all training women=213had diabetes÷614women in the box=35%half or less: call no

For Gini impurity worked out by hand, and the greedy search written in code, see A5, Decision trees, in the How AI Learns primer.

Analogy. A tree resembles a game of twenty questions.

Where it breaks. In the game you pick the next question as you go. A tree's questions are fixed when training ends, and it asks all women the same questions in the same order.

Rebuild the tree from memory

After training, what does the tree keep?

Its questions, cut-offs and the calls at its leaves. It keeps no copy of the women.

Deeper trees and overfitting

Guess first. You let the tree ask ten questions in a row instead of two. Will it do better on new women?

overfitting
learning quirks of the training women that new women do not share
smallest box
the fewest women a box may contain, which stops a tree from cutting out single women

Past a few questions, a deeper tree keeps improving on its training women and gets worse on new ones.

As the depth limit rises the boxes shrink and the training accuracy climbs, while the average held-out accuracy peaks at depth 3 and then falls.

These trees ask about glucose and BMI only, the two measurements of the last figure, and they grow by the same search. Drag the depth limit, or press Play to deepen the tree one level at a time. The boxes shrink until a box has a handful of women, and the training accuracy climbs from 77.2% at depth 2 to 93.2% at depth 12. The coral curve is the held-out accuracy of the project's split, and it is jumpy because 154 women are few. The dashed curve averages 50 other random splits of the 768 women. It peaks at 74.4% at depth 3 and falls to 68.1% at depth 12.

77.2%training−77.3%held out=−0.1gap, in points

The gap is the clearest sign of overfitting. It is near zero for shallow trees and reaches 19.8 points at depth 12. With all eight measurements it grows to about 30 points. Averaged over 50 random splits, that tree reaches 100% on its training women with no depth limit, while its held-out accuracy peaks at 74.7% at depth 2 and falls to 70.6%.

Try this. Set the depth limit to 12, then raise the smallest box from 1 to 30 women. The tree falls from 130 boxes to 15. Its training accuracy drops to 77.7% and its held-out accuracy rises to 77.3%, so the gap closes.

For depth, smallest boxes and held-out sets in more detail, see A5, Decision trees, in the How AI Learns primer.

Common belief. A model that is right about nearly all its training women will be right about new women too.

Why it fails. A depth-12 tree on these two measurements is right about 93% of its training women and 73% of the held-out women. With all eight measurements and no depth limit it is right about all its training women and wrong about three in ten new ones.

Better account. Pick settings such as depth with women the model did not train on, and report the grade on the held-out set.

Quick quiz

What is overfitting?

Learning quirks of the training women that new women do not share. Training accuracy rises while held-out accuracy falls.

Which depth would you pick from the figure, and why?

About two or three, where the average held-out accuracy peaks. Deeper trees only raise the training score.

Boosting: many small trees

Guess first. One small tree makes mistakes. What if a second tree studied only those mistakes, and a third the mistakes left after that?

boosting
adding small trees one after another, with a new tree reducing the errors left so far
learning rate
how far a new tree moves the scores
stump
a tree with a single question
leaf penalty
a number added to a leaf's count, which shrinks the answer of a leaf with few women
XGBoost
a popular library for boosting

Boosting builds a strong model from many weak trees by letting a new tree correct what the earlier trees got wrong.

With glucose alone, the staircase of scores climbs as stumps are added and follows the share of women with diabetes at that glucose. The gaps in the lower plot cannot vanish, because a label is either 0 or 1.

How does a new tree know what to fix? It looks at the gap between a woman's label and her current score. Before any tree, all women score 0.347, the training average. A woman with diabetes has a gap of 1 − 0.347 = 0.653, and a woman without has a gap of −0.347. The lower plot shows these two rows of gaps. A new stump sorts the training women into two leaves by their glucose. A leaf answers with the sum of its women's gaps divided by their number plus the leaf penalty, which is close to their average gap when the leaf is large. The score then moves by a small part of that answer, set by the learning rate:

new score=old score+ηlearning rate×leaf answerclose to the average gap in her leaf

Press Step or Play, and pick Woman A, B or C under "Equation boxes follow". The boxes under the plots give that woman's gap, her leaf's answer and her new score, tree by tree. Woman A had diabetes and Woman B did not, so their gaps start with opposite signs.

At a learning rate of 0.01 the scores move slowly. The 0.5 line first appears after 34 trees, and after 100 trees the two-input model is right for 74.0% of the held-out women.

Now glucose and BMI work together, with the settings of the project's model: trees of depth 3, a learning rate of 0.01, a leaf penalty of 1 and 100 trees. The project's model also reads six more measurements, so this is a two-input version and its numbers differ. Press Play and watch the map of scores sharpen. The black line is where the score reaches 0.5, the cut-off for calling diabetes. Follow Woman A, the star on the map:

Woman A's scoreafter 0 trees=0.347start++0.000sum of the trees' steps=0.347her score

With a learning rate this small, the scores spread slowly. After 100 trees Woman A's score is 0.594, and the held-out scores run from 0.14 to 0.69. The project's model with eight inputs ends between about 0.15 and 0.70. Raise the learning rate to 0.1 and the map sharpens within about ten trees, but the scores overshoot, running from −0.02 to 1.13 after 100 trees.

For boosting on residuals step by step, the leaf penalty and four ways to ask which inputs matter, see A6, Ensembles and boosting, in the How AI Learns primer.

Analogy. Boosting resembles a row of editors passing a draft along, with the next editor fixing what the last one missed.

Where it breaks. An editor can rewrite a whole sentence. A tree only shifts scores by a small step set by the learning rate, so no single tree fixes much.

Quick quiz

What does the learning rate do in boosting?

It scales the step a new tree adds to the scores. A small rate needs more trees and tends to fit noise less.

Why do the scores start at 0.347?

It is the share of training women with diabetes, the best single guess before any tree.

A neural network: one unit

Guess first. A unit multiplies glucose by one weight and BMI by another, adds them up, and keeps the result only if it is positive. Where on the glucose and BMI plot will it switch on?

unit
the building block of a neural network
weight
how strongly a unit counts one input
bias
a number added at the end, which shifts where the unit switches on
activation
the function a unit applies to its weighted sum, which sets how the output follows the sum
ReLU
keep a positive sum, turn a negative sum into zero

A unit with two inputs splits the plot along a straight line, and its activation decides what the output does on either side.

The weights tilt the dashed line where the weighted sum is zero, the bias moves it, and the activation sets how the output grows away from it. Coral marks a high output, navy a low one, and the star is the input being read.

The inputs are scaled first, so that glucose and BMI have similar sizes. Here is Woman A's glucose, the star at the start:

scaled glucose=(158her glucose−120.9training average)÷32.0spread=1.16

Her BMI of 31.6 is close to the training average of 32.0, so it scales to −0.05. The unit then weighs the two, adds the bias and applies its activation, which starts as ReLU:

0.70her output=ReLU(1.50weight×1.16glucose+0.80weight×−0.05BMI+−1.00bias)

Move the sliders and watch her output change. A larger weight on glucose makes the unit care more about glucose, which tilts the line. When the sum inside the brackets turns negative, ReLU outputs zero and the unit is off for her. This equation always follows Woman A. The star and the boxes inside the figure follow the input sliders, or a click on the map, so press the button to bring the star back to her.

The menu offers six activations. With none, the output is the weighted sum itself. A step gives 1 when the sum is 0 or more and 0 otherwise. A sigmoid squashes the sum into a number between 0 and 1, and tanh into a number between −1 and 1. ReLU keeps a positive sum and turns a negative one into 0, and GELU is a smooth version of ReLU. The left plot draws the chosen curve in navy and its slope as a dashed sand curve, with the star's sum marked on both.

The dots on the map are the 614 training women, and a ringed dot is a woman the unit calls wrongly. The readout counts the women called correctly, and always answering no is right for 65.3% of them. Press Train and the computer nudges the three dials to cut the error, the way the next section describes for the whole network. With a sigmoid the dials settle near 1.11, 0.65 and −0.83, and the unit calls 468 of the 614 women correctly, 76.2%. Reset the dials and train with the other activations. They end between 75.7% and 76.9%, and the step wobbles around the same level. The choice changes little here, because a unit with two inputs draws only one straight line.

Try this. Set the weight on BMI to 0. What happens to the line, and why?

Answer

The line turns vertical. The unit now ignores BMI, so only glucose decides whether it switches on.

For the full neuron playground, the perceptron rule and a layer of neurons, see B1, One neuron, in the How AI Learns primer.

Quick quiz

What does ReLU do?

It passes a positive sum on and turns a negative sum into zero.

What changes when you change the bias?

The line moves without tilting.

What changes when you swap the activation?

How the output follows the weighted sum. The line where the sum is zero stays where it is.

Layers and training

Guess first. The app's network contains 27,452 numbers. Who chooses them?

layer
a row of units that all read the same inputs
embedding
a short list of learned numbers that represents a category, such as an age
gradient
which way, and how steeply, the error changes when a weight changes
epoch
one pass through all the training women

Training sets all 27,452 numbers together, by nudging them again and again to shrink the error on the training women.

A prediction flows from left to right, and in a layer only some units switch on.

Pick a woman and press Run the model. The figure draws 10 of the 200 units in layer 1 and 8 of the 100 in layer 2. They are units that switch on for about half of the women. A filled unit is switched on, and a darker fill means a larger output. An empty circle is a unit that ReLU switched off for this woman.

A unit in the second layer reads the outputs of the first, so it can respond to bends and corners built from many lines. Age and the number of pregnancies, drawn as squares, enter as categories through embeddings.

The final unit adds up the second layer into one number, called z. A squashing function, the sigmoid, turns any z into a number between 0 and 1, and the model stretches that to the range 0 to 1.2:

…her score=1.2top of the range×sigmoid(…z, the final sum)
sigmoid(z)=1÷(1+e−ze is about 2.718)

A large positive z gives a sigmoid close to 1, a large negative z gives one close to 0, and z = 0 gives one half.

Training measures the error, which is how far scores fall from labels, squared and averaged over the training women. Here is the term for the woman you picked:

(…her score−…her label)2=…her error term

Training then works out the gradient. For a weight, it says which direction of change would shrink the average error, and how steeply. Training moves all 27,452 weights a small step in that direction, then repeats:

new weight=old weight−learning rate×gradient

This model made 11 passes through the training women, in batches of 128. You can watch the units respond to your own numbers in Try the model on the project page.

Analogy. Layers resemble stages on an assembly line.

Where it breaks. Training adjusts all stages at once, using the error measured at the end of the line.

Common belief. The app keeps learning from the numbers people type in.

Why it fails. Training finished before the app went online. The 27,452 numbers stay fixed whatever you type.

Better account. A deployed model changes only when someone retrains it on new data.

Quick quiz

Who sets the 27,452 numbers?

Training does, through many small steps that reduce the error on the training women. Nobody writes them by hand.

Does the app learn from what you type?

No. Its numbers stay fixed until someone retrains it.

What is an embedding?

A short list of learned numbers for a category, such as one age.

From a score to a decision

Guess first. For a screening test, would you rather miss a case or raise a false alarm?

sensitivity
the share of women with diabetes that the test catches
specificity
the share of women without diabetes that the test clears
calibration
whether a score of 0.6 matches a 60% rate in the data

Lowering the cut-off catches more women with diabetes and clears fewer women without it.

No cut-off separates the two rows cleanly, so any choice trades misses against false alarms.

The dots are the 154 held-out women, placed by the rebuilt model's score. The right balance depends on the cost of a missed case against the cost of a follow-up test.

sensitivity=29caught÷(29caught+26missed)=52.7%
specificity=92cleared÷(92cleared+7false alarms)=92.9%

Common belief. A score of 0.6 means a 60% chance of diabetes.

Why it fails. Training pushed scores toward 0 or 1, and nobody checked whether a 0.6 matches a 60% rate. The network's scores even run up to 1.2.

Better account. Read the score as a ranking. Check calibration before reading it as a chance.

Quick quiz

Define sensitivity and specificity.

Sensitivity is the share of women with diabetes the test catches. Specificity is the share without diabetes that it clears.

Why is the score here not a probability?

The models were trained to push scores toward 0 or 1, and calibration was never checked.

Counting questions can mislead

Guess first. The rebuilt model's trees ask about blood pressure 52 times. Is blood pressure one of its most important inputs?

feature importance
any measure of how much a model relies on an input
split count
how often the trees ask about an input; the report's F-score chart uses it
shuffle test
shuffle one input among the held-out women and see how much accuracy drops

An input can be asked about often and still change almost no calls.

The trees ask about blood pressure and insulin around 50 times, and shuffling them costs no accuracy.

Shuffling one input among the held-out women keeps its values but breaks their link to the outcome. If accuracy drops, the model was using that input. The test repeats the shuffle 50 times and averages the drop. For glucose:

18.4points lost=78.6%accuracy as it is−60.2%with glucose shuffled

Shuffling blood pressure or insulin costs no accuracy, although the trees ask about them often, probably to make small adjustments that cancel out.

Common belief. The input a model asks about most often matters most.

Why it fails. A count measures habit. Blood pressure gets 52 questions and no measurable effect.

Better account. Measure effect directly, with a shuffle test or SHAP values.

Quick quiz

Why can a split count mislead?

It counts how often the trees ask about an input, which can differ from how much the input changes the calls.

When can a shuffle test underrate an input?

When two inputs share the same information. Shuffle one, and the model leans on the other, so the drop looks small.

Sharing out a score

Guess first. A woman's score is 0.67 and the average score is 0.35. How would you split the extra 0.32 fairly between her glucose, BMI and age?

SHAP value
an input's fair share of the gap between a score and the average score
Shapley value
the game-theory idea behind it: a player's average added effect over all orders of joining

An input's fair share is its average added effect over all the orders in which the inputs could be revealed.

Glucose adds a different amount depending on when it arrives, and averaging over orders settles its share.

This toy uses three inputs and made-up numbers so that the sums stay visible. The left table gives the score for any set of known inputs. Click an order to build the score in that order. The bottom row averages the six orders, and the three averages add up to 0.32.

0.67her score=0.35average score+0.215glucose+0.070BMI+0.035age

For trees, an algorithm called TreeSHAP computes these averages without listing all the orders, so it stays fast with eight inputs. The project page runs it in your browser in Explain one score.

Quick quiz

What is a SHAP value?

An input's average added effect on the score over all the orders in which the inputs could be revealed.

Why do the SHAP values add up to the score minus the average?

Along any one order, the added effects sum to the full score minus the average. Averaging over orders keeps that sum.

Where the model can fail

Guess first. Could a clinic in Mumbai use this model as it is?

dataset shift
new people who differ from the people in the training data
missing value
a measurement that was never taken

A model knows only the people in its data, and several columns of this data record "not measured" as zero.

Almost half the women have an insulin value of zero, which means the test was not done.

A glucose level, blood pressure or BMI of zero is impossible, so a zero means the measurement is missing. A model that reads those zeros as low values learns from how the data was recorded. The usual remedy is to mark them as missing so the model can treat "missing" as its own case.

The study covers women of Pima heritage in the United States, aged 21 or older. Men, younger people and other populations differ in base rates and in their measurements, so the model could miss cases or raise false alarms for them.

Claim, evidence, limit. Fill these in for the app before you open the answer.

A model answer

Claim. The app flags women likely to have diabetes. Evidence. The rebuilt model is right for 78.6% of 154 held-out women from the same study. Limit. People unlike the study's women, such as men, and records where a zero means "not measured".

Think about it

Who is missing from this data, and who could be harmed if a clinic used the model anyway?

Men, people under 21 and other populations are missing. A clinic serving them could see missed cases or needless worry. Testing on local data comes first, and retraining probably follows.

Why are the zeros a problem?

A zero glucose, blood pressure or BMI is impossible, so it means "not measured". A model that reads it as a low value learns from a recording habit.

Answer these in two days, and again in a week. Testing yourself after a gap helps far more than rereading. They mix in ideas from the other primers on this site.

In boosting, the learning rate sets how far a new tree moves the scores. What does it set in the optimizer primer?

How far the optimizer moves the weights in one step. In both, a small rate means many careful steps.

The TTT primer describes a model that changes some of its weights while it reads. Does the diabetes app do anything like that?

No. The app's numbers stay fixed after training. In-Place TTT is designed to change a few weights during reading, which is why it needs its own primer.

Why must the held-out set stay untouched while you choose settings such as tree depth?

If you pick settings by their held-out score, you tune the model to those women, and the held-out grade becomes too kind, like a training score.
