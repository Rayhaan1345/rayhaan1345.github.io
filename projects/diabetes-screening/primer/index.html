What are we predicting?

Guess first. A model answers "no diabetes" for all 768 women in the data, without looking at a single measurement. Could it still be right most of the time?

model
a recipe that turns measurements into a score
label
the known answer for a woman in the data, diabetes or not
base rate
the share of women whose label is diabetes

A useful model has to beat the lazy answer, and here the lazy answer is right 65% of the time.

A third of the women have diabetes, so saying "no" to all of them is right for the other two thirds.

The data comes from a study of 768 women aged 21 or older. Glucose, BMI, blood pressure and five other things were measured, and the study recorded who had diabetes. Of the 768, 268 had diabetes, a base rate of 34.9%.

Switch to "always say no". The lazy model is right for 65.1% of the women and catches none of the 268 with diabetes. A grade of 65% sounds fine and hides a useless screening test.

Common belief. A model that is right two times in three has learned something.

Why it fails. The lazy model reaches 65% without reading any measurement.

Better account. Compare a model with the base rate, and count missed cases on their own.

Quick quiz

What is the base rate here, and why does it matter?

It is 34.9%. Any model has to beat the 65.1% that a lazy "no" gets.

Why is accuracy alone a poor grade for a screening test?

A model can score well while missing most women with diabetes, as the lazy model shows.

Grading on women the model never saw

Guess first. You prepare for an exam with a practice paper that comes with its answers. You score 100% on the practice paper. What does that tell you about your exam score?

training set
the women the model learns from
held-out set
women kept aside and used only to grade the model
accuracy
the share of women the model calls correctly

You grade a model on women it never saw while learning, because its score on the women it learned from is too kind.

The split sets aside 154 women at random, and both groups keep about the same share with diabetes.

Before any learning, the project set aside 154 of the 768 women. The model learns from the other 614 and is graded on the 154. Choosing the held-out women at random keeps the grade fair, because the two groups look alike.

Analogy. The training set is the practice paper with answers. The held-out set is the exam.

Where it breaks. An exam asks new questions on the same course. The held-out women come from the same study, so a good grade says little about women from a different population. The last section returns to this.

Quick quiz

Why can't you grade the model on its training women?

It has seen their answers, so its score there is too kind and says little about new women.

When do you choose the held-out set?

Before any learning or tuning. If you use it to pick settings, it stops being a fair test.

One rule

Guess first. You may ask one question about one measurement. Which measurement would you pick, and where would you draw the line?

cut-off
the value above which the rule calls diabetes
caught, missed
women with diabetes the rule calls yes, or calls no
false alarm, cleared
women without diabetes the rule calls yes, or calls no

A single cut-off on glucose is already a model, and it gets about three in four held-out women right.

Women with diabetes tend to have higher glucose, so one line already sorts most of them.

Drag the line or use the slider. The shaded side is called diabetes, and the four counts are the four outcomes of any yes-or-no test. The best cut-off on the training women is 144 mg/dL, and it is right for 75.3% of the held-out women.

Try this. Move the line left until the rule catches most women with diabetes. Watch the false alarms.

Where it breaks. One rule reads one measurement. Two women with the same glucose get the same call, whatever their BMI or age.

The trade-off. Moving the line left catches more cases and raises more false alarms. For screening, a missed case usually costs more than a false alarm, which argues for a lower cut-off than the most accurate one.

Quick quiz

Name the four outcomes of a yes-or-no test.

Caught, missed, false alarm and cleared.

Why might a screening test use a lower cut-off than the most accurate one?

A missed case usually costs more than a false alarm, which a follow-up test can sort out.

Decision trees

Guess first. A woman's glucose is high. Which second question would help you decide?

decision tree
questions in a chain, where the next question depends on the last answer
leaf
the end of a path, where the tree makes its call
depth
how many questions the tree asks in a row
training
searching for the questions that sort the training women best

A tree chains questions, and training it means searching for the questions that sort the training women best.

    A tree with two questions splits the plot into four boxes, and the box decides the call.

    This tree asks about glucose first and BMI second. Coral dots had diabetes and navy dots did not. A box turns coral when most of its training women had diabetes, and the number in a box is that share. Press "Let the computer choose" to run the search that training performs.

    Analogy. A tree resembles a game of twenty questions.

    Where it breaks. In the game you pick the next question as you go. A tree's questions are fixed when training ends, and it asks all women the same questions in the same order.

    Rebuild the tree from memory

    After training, what does the tree keep?

    Its questions, cut-offs and the calls at its leaves. It keeps no copy of the women.

    Deeper trees and overfitting

    Guess first. You let the tree ask ten questions in a row instead of two. Will it do better on new women?

    overfitting
    learning quirks of the training women that new women do not share

    Past a few questions, a deeper tree keeps improving on its training women and gets worse on new ones.

    Training accuracy climbs to 100% as the tree deepens, while held-out accuracy drifts down from its peak near depth 2.

    These trees use all eight measurements. A deep tree carves the women into many small boxes. With no depth limit it makes about 109 leaves for 614 women, about six women per leaf, so it has in effect memorised them. The curves average 50 random splits, because one held-out set of 154 women is noisy. The band shows where most of those splits fall.

    Common belief. A model that is right about nearly all its training women will be right about new women too.

    Why it fails. The deepest tree is right about all its training women and wrong about three in ten new ones.

    Better account. Pick settings such as depth with women the model did not train on, and report the grade on the held-out set.

    Quick quiz

    What is overfitting?

    Learning quirks of the training women that new women do not share. Training accuracy rises while held-out accuracy falls.

    Which depth would you pick from the figure, and why?

    About two or three, where held-out accuracy peaks. Deeper trees only raise the training score.

    Boosting: many small trees

    Guess first. One small tree makes mistakes. What if a second tree studied only those mistakes, and a third the mistakes left after that?

    boosting
    adding small trees one after another, with a new tree reducing the errors left so far
    learning rate
    how far a new tree moves the scores
    XGBoost
    a popular library for boosting

    Boosting builds a strong model from many weak trees by letting a new tree correct what the earlier trees got wrong.

    As trees are added, women with diabetes drift right and women without drift left.

    The model rebuilt from the project report uses 100 trees of depth 3 and a learning rate of 0.01. It starts all women at the training average, 0.347, and the trees move their scores from there. Press Play to add trees. With a learning rate this small, the scores spread slowly and end between about 0.15 and 0.70.

    Analogy. Boosting resembles a row of editors passing a draft along, with the next editor fixing what the last one missed.

    Where it breaks. An editor can rewrite a whole sentence. A tree only shifts scores by a small step set by the learning rate, so no single tree fixes much.

    Quick quiz

    What does the learning rate do in boosting?

    It scales the step a new tree adds to the scores. A small rate needs more trees and tends to fit noise less.

    Why do the scores start at 0.347?

    It is the share of training women with diabetes, the best single guess before any tree.

    A neural network: one unit

    Guess first. A unit multiplies glucose by one weight and BMI by another, adds them up, and keeps the result only if it is positive. Where on the glucose and BMI plot will it switch on?

    unit
    the building block of a neural network
    weight
    how strongly a unit counts one input
    bias
    a number added at the end, which shifts where the unit switches on
    ReLU
    keep a positive sum, turn a negative sum into zero

    A unit with two inputs splits the plot along a straight line: active on one side, zero on the other.

    The weights tilt the dashed line and the bias moves it, and darker teal marks a larger output.

    The inputs are scaled first: subtract the training average and divide by the spread, so glucose and BMI have similar sizes. A larger weight on glucose makes the unit care more about glucose, which tilts the line.

    Try this. Set the weight on BMI to 0. What happens to the line, and why?

    Answer

    The line turns vertical. The unit now ignores BMI, so only glucose decides whether it switches on.

    Quick quiz

    What does ReLU do?

    It passes a positive sum on and turns a negative sum into zero.

    What changes when you change the bias?

    The line moves without tilting.

    Layers and training

    Guess first. The app's network contains 27,452 numbers. Who chooses them?

    layer
    a row of units that all read the same inputs
    embedding
    a short list of learned numbers that represents a category, such as an age
    gradient
    which way, and how steeply, the error changes when a weight changes
    epoch
    one pass through all the training women

    Training sets all 27,452 numbers together, by nudging them again and again to shrink the error on the training women.

    Measurements enter on the left and a single score leaves on the right, after two layers of units.

    A unit in the second layer reads the outputs of the first, so it can respond to bends and corners built from many lines. The final unit combines the second layer into one number, and a squashing step, the sigmoid, maps it into the range 0 to 1.2. Age and the number of pregnancies enter as categories, through embeddings.

    Training measures the error, which is the average squared gap between a woman's score and her label, 0 or 1. It works out the gradient for all weights and moves them a small step downhill. This model trained for 11 epochs, in batches of 128 women. You can watch the units respond in Try the model on the project page.

    Analogy. Layers resemble stages on an assembly line.

    Where it breaks. Training adjusts all stages at once, using the error measured at the end of the line.

    Common belief. The app keeps learning from the numbers people type in.

    Why it fails. Training finished before the app went online. The 27,452 numbers stay fixed whatever you type.

    Better account. A deployed model changes only when someone retrains it on new data.

    Quick quiz

    Who sets the 27,452 numbers?

    Training does, through many small steps that reduce the error on the training women. Nobody writes them by hand.

    Does the app learn from what you type?

    No. Its numbers stay fixed until someone retrains it.

    What is an embedding?

    A short list of learned numbers for a category, such as one age.

    From a score to a decision

    Guess first. For a screening test, would you rather miss a case or raise a false alarm?

    sensitivity
    the share of women with diabetes that the test catches
    specificity
    the share of women without diabetes that the test clears
    calibration
    whether a score of 0.6 matches a 60% rate in the data

    Lowering the cut-off catches more women with diabetes and clears fewer women without it.

    No cut-off separates the two rows cleanly, so any choice trades misses against false alarms.

    The dots are the 154 held-out women, placed by the rebuilt model's score. The right balance depends on the cost of a missed case against the cost of a follow-up test.

    Common belief. A score of 0.6 means a 60% chance of diabetes.

    Why it fails. Training pushed scores toward 0 or 1, and nobody checked whether a 0.6 matches a 60% rate. The network's scores even run up to 1.2.

    Better account. Read the score as a ranking. Check calibration before reading it as a chance.

    Quick quiz

    Define sensitivity and specificity.

    Sensitivity is the share of women with diabetes the test catches. Specificity is the share without diabetes that it clears.

    Why is the score here not a probability?

    The models were trained to push scores toward 0 or 1, and calibration was never checked.

    Counting questions can mislead

    Guess first. The rebuilt model's trees ask about blood pressure 52 times. Is blood pressure one of its most important inputs?

    feature importance
    any measure of how much a model relies on an input
    split count
    how often the trees ask about an input; the report's F-score chart uses it
    shuffle test
    shuffle one input among the held-out women and see how much accuracy drops

    An input can be asked about often and still change almost no calls.

    The trees ask about blood pressure and insulin around 50 times, and shuffling them costs no accuracy.

    Shuffling one input among the held-out women keeps its values but breaks their link to the outcome. If accuracy drops, the model was using that input. Glucose loses the most, about 18 points. Shuffling blood pressure or insulin costs no accuracy, although the trees ask about them often, probably to make small adjustments that cancel out.

    Common belief. The input a model asks about most often matters most.

    Why it fails. A count measures habit. Blood pressure gets 52 questions and no measurable effect.

    Better account. Measure effect directly, with a shuffle test or SHAP values.

    Quick quiz

    Why can a split count mislead?

    It counts how often the trees ask about an input, which can differ from how much the input changes the calls.

    When can a shuffle test underrate an input?

    When two inputs share the same information. Shuffle one, and the model leans on the other, so the drop looks small.

    Sharing out a score

    Guess first. A woman's score is 0.67 and the average score is 0.35. How would you split the extra 0.32 fairly between her glucose, BMI and age?

    SHAP value
    an input's fair share of the gap between a score and the average score
    Shapley value
    the game-theory idea behind it: a player's average added effect over all orders of joining

    An input's fair share is its average added effect over all the orders in which the inputs could be revealed.

    Glucose adds a different amount depending on when it arrives, and averaging over orders settles its share.

    This toy uses three inputs and made-up numbers so that the sums stay visible. The left table gives the score for any set of known inputs. Click an order to build the score in that order. The bottom row averages the six orders, and the three averages add up to 0.32.

    For trees, an algorithm called TreeSHAP computes these averages without listing all the orders, so it stays fast with eight inputs. The project page runs it in your browser in Explain one score.

    Quick quiz

    What is a SHAP value?

    An input's average added effect on the score over all the orders in which the inputs could be revealed.

    Why do the SHAP values add up to the score minus the average?

    Along any one order, the added effects sum to the full score minus the average. Averaging over orders keeps that sum.

    Where the model can fail

    Guess first. Could a clinic in Mumbai use this model as it is?

    dataset shift
    new people who differ from the people in the training data
    missing value
    a measurement that was never taken

    A model knows only the people in its data, and several columns of this data record "not measured" as zero.

    Almost half the women have an insulin value of zero, which means the test was not done.

    A glucose level, blood pressure or BMI of zero is impossible, so a zero means the measurement is missing. A model that reads those zeros as low values learns from how the data was recorded. The usual remedy is to mark them as missing so the model can treat "missing" as its own case.

    The study covers women of Pima heritage in the United States, aged 21 or older. Men, younger people and other populations differ in base rates and in their measurements, so the model could miss cases or raise false alarms for them.

    Claim, evidence, limit. Fill these in for the app before you open the answer.

    A model answer

    Claim. The app flags women likely to have diabetes. Evidence. The rebuilt model is right for 78.6% of 154 held-out women from the same study. Limit. People unlike the study's women, such as men, and records where a zero means "not measured".

    Think about it

    Who is missing from this data, and who could be harmed if a clinic used the model anyway?

    Men, people under 21 and other populations are missing. A clinic serving them could see missed cases or needless worry. Testing on local data comes first, and retraining probably follows.

    Why are the zeros a problem?

    A zero glucose, blood pressure or BMI is impossible, so it means "not measured". A model that reads it as a low value learns from a recording habit.
