A Primer on In-Place Test-Time Training

From attention to the fast-weight update, one step at a time

Rayhaan Khan · Mumbai AI Lab

Companion to the study In-Place Test-Time Training on Qwen3-4B

This page builds In-Place TTT up from the ground. It starts with how a language model reads text and goes through attention and the MLP. It ends with the update the method applies, including where its gradient comes from and when it runs.

The only background needed is school algebra. A vector is a list of numbers, and a matrix is a grid of numbers that turns one vector into another. The demos use small made-up numbers. Statements about the method follow the released code, and the last section points to the lines that implement them.

Text becomes numbers

A language model reads text as tokens. A token is a word or a piece of a word, and the model swaps it for a list of numbers called a vector. In Qwen3-4B, the model in the study, this vector has 2,560 numbers.

The vectors then pass up through a stack of 36 layers. A layer reads the vector of a token and adds a correction to it. Think of the vector as a notepad that the layers keep writing on. This running notepad is called the residual stream.

At the top, the model turns the notepad of the last token into a score for all possible next tokens. Training adjusts the weights so that the correct next token gets a high score. The weights are the numbers inside the layers' matrices, about 4 billion of them in Qwen3-4B.

From text to a guess. A token becomes a vector, the layers add to it, and the top turns the last vector into scores for the next token. The colours show made-up numbers, teal for positive and coral for negative.

For how a tokenizer learns its pieces, and why some languages cost more tokens, see D1, Tokenisation, in the How AI Learns primer.

Attention: looking back

To guess what comes next, a token often needs facts from earlier in the text. Take “The key is under the mat. The key”. To continue, the last “key” should find the earlier sentence about the key. Attention is the step that lets a token look back and collect what it needs.

Attention gives a token three vectors, made by multiplying its notepad by three learned matrices.

  • Query: what this token is looking for.
  • Key: what this token offers, like a label on a folder.
  • Value: what this token hands over when it is picked.

A token compares its query with its own key and with the keys of the tokens before it. The comparison is a dot product: multiply matching entries of the two vectors and add the results. A large dot product means a good match.

Softmax turns these scores into weights that are positive and add up to 1. The output of attention is the sum of the values, weighted by these weights. For the token at position i:

aij = softmaxj( qi · kj / √d )outputi = Σj ≤ i aij vj

Here d is the length of a key vector. Dividing by √d keeps the scores in a sensible range when d is large.

The causal mask

A model that learns to predict the next token must not see it in advance. So a token looks at itself and at earlier tokens only. Before softmax, the scores for later tokens are set to minus infinity, which turns their weights into zero. This is the causal mask, and it makes the grid of weights triangular.

Attention on a toy sentence. Tap a token or a row of the grid to make it the one doing the looking. Bars show the weights it gives to the tokens it can see. In the grid, row i shows the weights of token i. The vectors are made up and have four numbers. Switching the mask off lets tokens see the future, which a trained model never does. The slider multiplies the scores, so a sharper setting puts more weight on the best match.

Heads

The model runs attention as several heads side by side. A head has its own query, key and value matrices, so different heads can look for different things. A final matrix mixes the outputs of the heads back into one vector for the notepad.

For queries, keys and values worked by hand, and the causal mask, see D4, Self-attention, in the How AI Learns primer.

Generating text and the KV cache

The model writes one token at a time. It reads the prompt, picks a next token, adds it to the text and repeats. This happens in two phases.

  • Prefill reads the whole prompt in one pass, with all positions computed side by side.
  • Decoding then produces the answer, one token per step.

In a decoding step, the newest token needs the keys and values of all earlier tokens, in all 36 layers. Those keys and values never change, because earlier tokens never look at later ones. So the model computes them once and stores them. The store is called the KV cache.

With the cache, a step computes the query, key and value of the newest token alone. It appends the new key and value to the cache, then attends over the cache. The cache grows by one entry per token in all layers, which is why long inputs need a lot of memory.

The transformer block and its MLP

A layer of the model is also called a block. It has two parts, attention and an MLP, and both add their result onto the notepad. Before both parts, a step called RMSNorm rescales the notepad to a standard size. Qwen3-4B stacks 36 of these blocks.

Attention moves information between positions. The MLP works on one position at a time. It takes the 2,560 numbers of a token's notepad, expands them to 9,728 numbers, applies a nonlinear step and compresses the result back to 2,560 numbers.

In Qwen3 the MLP has three matrices.

  1. The up projection U and another projection G both map the MLP input x, with 2,560 numbers, to 9,728 numbers. In the code, G is named gate_proj.
  2. The output of G goes through SiLU, a smooth function that keeps large positive values and pushes negative values toward zero. The result multiplies the output of U entry by entry. This gives the hidden vector h, with 9,728 numbers.
  3. The down projection W, a matrix with 2,560 rows and 9,728 columns, maps h back to 2,560 numbers.

h = SiLU(G x) ⊙ (U x)MLP(x) = W h

The symbol ⊙ means multiplying two vectors entry by entry.

One block of Qwen3-4B. Step through the parts, or tap one. The down projection W, in teal, is the matrix that In-Place TTT turns into a fast weight in six of the 36 blocks.

The down projection as a memory

There is a useful way to read W h. Column j of W is a vector of 2,560 numbers. The entry hj says how strongly hidden unit j responds to this token. The output W h is the sum of the columns, with column j scaled by hj.

So W stores 9,728 output directions, and h picks a mix of them. A hidden unit that responds to some pattern in the input adds its column to the notepad. For this reason, MLP layers have been described as key-value memories (Geva et al., 2021).

W h as a weighted sum of columns. A toy W with six rows and four columns. Move the sliders to change h. The output on the right is the sum of the scaled columns.

Writing into a matrix

If W h reads a mix of columns, then adding a matrix to W can store something new. Take a pattern h and a target v. Their outer product v h⊤ is a matrix whose entry in row i and column j is vi hj. Add it to W:

W′ = W + v h⊤

Now read W′ with a pattern g. The result is W′ g = W g + v (h · g). The new part is the target v, scaled by the dot product of the stored pattern and the reading pattern.

  • If g equals h, the output moves toward v, scaled by the squared length of h.
  • If g points in a similar direction to h, part of v comes back.
  • If g is at right angles to h, the dot product is zero and the output does not change.

Writing is adding outer products, and reading is the ordinary matrix product. A matrix used this way is called a fast weight. It changes quickly while the slow, trained weights stay put. The idea is older than transformers (Ba et al., 2016), and linear attention can be read in the same terms (Schlag et al., 2021).

How much comes back. Here h and g have length 1, so h · g is the cosine of the angle between them. The part of the target that comes back follows that cosine: all of it at 0°, none at 90° and its opposite at 180°. The study page has a matrix version of this demo.

Which weight In-Place TTT changes

In-Place TTT turns the down projection W into a fast weight in six of the 36 blocks: layers 0, 6, 12, 18, 24 and 30, counting from 0. All other weights stay fixed while the model reads. That includes the attention and the other two MLP matrices of those six blocks.

The method reuses the down projection because it already works as a memory of the kind above. No new layer goes between the existing ones, which is what “in place” refers to.

A down projection in Qwen3-4B has 2,560 × 9,728 entries, about 24.9 million numbers. The six fast weights together come to about 149 million of the model's 4.0 billion parameters.

The method also adds two small trained parts to these six blocks to make the targets: a convolution and a projection P. They are trained along with the model and stay fixed at test time.

The saved model never changes. At the start of an input, the code copies the trained down projection, called W0, and updates the copy. The next input starts again from W0.

Where the fast weights are. Pick one of the six layers to see how far its W moved on held-out 32k text in the study. The numbers are the Frobenius norm of W − W0 after the first and the seventh update, measured on eight held-out sequences. The size of W0 is between 112 and 126 on the same scale.

The target, the loss and the gradient

Here is the full update in four steps. It happens in the six fast-weight layers, once per complete chunk of 4,096 tokens.

  1. Patternsthe hidden vectors ht the MLP computes anyway
  2. Targetsa short convolution of the MLP input, then the projection P
  3. Losshow well the outputs W ht agree with the targets
  4. Stepone gradient step on W alone

Step 1: the patterns

While the layer reads the chunk, the MLP computes the hidden vector ht for token t, as in any model. These vectors of 9,728 numbers are the patterns. In-Place TTT needs no extra work to get them.

Step 2: the targets

The target starts from xt, the vector the MLP reads after attention and RMSNorm. A convolution mixes xt with its neighbours over a window of five positions: two before, the token itself and two after.

The convolution is depthwise. Dimension k of the result mixes dimension k of the five neighbours only, with five learned weights for that dimension. The window stops at the edges of the chunk.

The projection P, a learned matrix of 2,560 × 2,560 numbers, then mixes the dimensions. The result is the target v̂t. It has 2,560 numbers, the same size as the layer's output.

v̂t = P⊤ conv(x)t

Because the window reaches two tokens ahead, the target contains information about the tokens that come next. That links the update to the model's job, predicting the next token.

Looking ahead does not leak answers. The update from a chunk is applied after the whole chunk has been read, so it changes the outputs of later chunks only.

Step 3: the loss

The output of the layer for token t is W ht. The update uses a simple loss: minus the sum, over the chunk, of the dot products between outputs and targets.

L(W) = − Σt v̂t · (W ht)

An output that points the same way as its target lowers the loss. No labels are involved. The targets come from the text itself, which makes this self-supervised learning.

Step 4: one gradient step

The gradient of a loss with respect to W says how the loss changes when the entries of W change. For this loss it has a short closed form. Only W is varied here. P, the convolution and the rest of the network are held fixed.

∂L / ∂W = − Σt v̂t ht⊤

Gradient descent moves W a step of size η against the gradient:

W ← W + η Σt v̂t ht⊤

This is the memory write from “Writing into a matrix”, done for all tokens of the chunk and added up. With v̂t written out, it is the update in the paper, W ← W + η Σt (P⊤ conv(x)t) ht⊤. The released code computes the sum directly with one matrix product. No backpropagation through the network runs at test time.

The step always lowers this loss on the chunk it came from. The drop equals η times the sum of the squares of the entries of Σt v̂t ht⊤. The loss is linear in W, though, so it has no lowest point. A bigger η lowers it further without limit, and the loss sets no bound on how far W moves. The step size η decides that.

When the update runs

The update runs in the six fast-weight layers, once per complete chunk of 4,096 tokens. Generation never stops to wait for it. What happens depends on the phase.

Prefill

The prompt is read in one forward pass. Inside a fast-weight layer, the code walks through the complete chunks of the prompt in order. For a chunk, it first computes the outputs with the current W, then updates W from that chunk. The next chunk is read with the new W.

A partial chunk left at the end is read with the latest W and gets no update yet. Its patterns and targets are kept in the cache.

Decoding

The pattern and target of a new token join the partial chunk in the cache. While the partial chunk is shorter than 4,096 tokens, the token is read with the current W. When the new token completes the chunk, the layer computes the update inside the same step. That token is read with the old W, and the tokens after it use the new one.

So once generation completes a chunk, the update also learns from text the model wrote itself.

Short and long prompts

A prompt shorter than 4,096 tokens triggers no update during prefill. RULER at 4k is such a case, so the study's 4k results measure the training effect alone. A prompt of 32,000 tokens contains seven complete chunks, or 28,672 tokens, and a partial one. Prefill then applies seven updates.

Training with the module

At test time only W moves, by the closed-form step. The other parts of the module are learned during training, when the model learns which notes are worth writing.

Training uses the usual next-token loss. The updates are part of the forward pass, so the gradient of that loss flows back through them. It reaches the convolution and P, which decide what gets written. It also reaches the shared weights, which decide the patterns.

For speed, training computes the updates of all chunks at once. W for chunk k is W0 plus η times the sum of the updates from the chunks before it. This running sum gives the same result as the chunk-by-chunk loop of inference.

Wk = W0 + η Σs < k ΔWsΔWs = Σt in chunk s v̂t ht⊤

At the start of training the convolution is all zeros. The targets and updates are then zero, so the model computes what the base model computes. In the released code, P starts as a diagonal matrix with small random entries.

This part of the method is where the study found the gains. Evaluated with the update off, the TTT-trained checkpoint keeps its lead over the control. Training with the module changed the shared weights, and that change explains the gains. The module also changed training itself. The gradient norm of the TTT arm was 100 to 1,000 times the control's, almost all of it in the six small convolutions. See how the module changes training on the study page.

The study's terms

Control
The same model, trained on the same data in the same order, with the module switched off.
TTT arm
The model trained with the module and evaluated as released, with η = 3.
Update off
The TTT checkpoint evaluated again with η = 0. The fast weights never leave W0, and the outputs match those of a model without the module.
Update effect
TTT minus update off, on the same questions. It measures adapting at test time.
Training effect
Update off minus control. It measures training with the module.
TTT minus control
The sum of the two effects. Averaged over seeds at 64k with plain RoPE, +3.2 = +0.1 + 3.1 RULER points. At 64k with YaRN, −1.7 = −2.5 + 0.8.
Both seeds
The study trained both arms twice, with two data orders called seeds 1 and 2. “Both seeds” averages the two per question, which is the estimate the decision rule uses.
Inner learning rate η
The step size of the test-time update: 3 in the released recipe. The study also tried 0, 1 and 6 at inference.
Chunk size
The number of tokens between updates: 4,096 as released. The study also trained with 512 and 1,024.
Clipping τ
A rule that shrinks the update of a chunk when its Frobenius norm is above τ. The paper reports τ = 10−5, while measured single-chunk updates have norms of 0.1 to 0.85. Clipping at τ shrinks them by a factor of 10,000 or more, which switches the update off in effect. The released code applies no clipping.
Frobenius norm
The size of a matrix: the square root of the sum of the squares of its entries.
RoPE and YaRN
RoPE encodes the position of a token by rotating its query and key vectors. YaRN stretches those rotations so that a model trained on 32k tokens can read 64k.
RULER
A benchmark of 13 long-context tasks, such as finding a planted fact in a long text.

Check yourself

Questions on the ideas above. Tap a question to see the answer.

Which weights change while the model reads?

The MLP down projections W in layers 0, 6, 12, 18, 24 and 30. All other weights stay fixed, including the convolution and P.

What is a target made from?

The MLP input x of the same layer. A depthwise convolution mixes it over five neighbouring positions, two before and two after, and the result is multiplied by P⊤.

Where does the gradient come from?

From the inner loss L(W) = −Σt v̂t · (W ht), differentiated with respect to W alone. The gradient is −Σt v̂t ht⊤, so the step is a sum of outer products. No backpropagation through the network runs at test time.

Does generation stop after 4,096 tokens to update W?

No. During prefill the updates run inside the single pass over the prompt. During decoding the update is a few matrix products inside the step that completes a chunk.

Can the update from a chunk help predict tokens inside the same chunk?

No. A chunk is read with the W from before its own update. That is why the targets can look two tokens ahead without leaking answers.

Why are the 4k results the same with the update on and off?

A 4k prompt is shorter than one chunk, so no update reaches the text the model reads. The 4k comparison with the control therefore measures the training effect alone.

What does η = 0 do?

It makes the update zero. W stays at W0, and the model computes what a model without the module computes. The study uses this to switch the update off without retraining.

Do fast weights stay from one document to the next?

No. A new input starts from W0, and the saved model never changes.

How are the convolution and P learned?

During training, through the next-token loss. The updates are part of the forward pass, so gradients flow through them into the convolution, P and the shared weights.

What does the KV cache store, and what does In-Place TTT add to it?

The keys and values of earlier tokens, in all layers. In the six fast-weight layers, In-Place TTT adds the patterns and targets of the partial chunk and the current W.

The step always lowers the inner loss. Why is that no guarantee of better answers?

The drop is η times the squared Frobenius norm of Σt v̂t ht⊤, which is never negative. The loss is linear in W and has no minimum, so η alone decides how far W moves. In the study, the unclipped update lowered accuracy at 64k with YaRN.

What did the study find?

Averaged over seeds, switching the update off changes the RULER mean by at most 0.4 points up to 64k with plain RoPE. At 64k with YaRN, the update costs 2.5 points. The gains over the control come from training with the module.

Where it lives in the code

The released implementation, at the commit the study pins, puts the method in two copies of the Qwen3 model file. Links open the lines on GitHub.

  • Qwen3MLP set-up: the projection ttt_proj (P), the step size ttt_lr (η) and the depthwise ttt_conv with a window of five and padding of two.
  • Qwen3MLP.forward, inference: the chunk loop. A chunk is read with the current weight, then dw is added if the chunk is complete.
  • Qwen3DecoderLayer.forward, inference: the target starts from the MLP input, and the partial chunk and current weight are kept in the cache between steps.
  • TTTDynamicCache: the KV cache with the extra fast-weight state for a layer.
  • Qwen3MLP.forward, training: all chunk updates at once, combined with a running sum, with gradients flowing through.
  • Initialisation: the convolution starts at zero and P as a diagonal matrix with small random entries.
  • The study's configuration: layers 0, 6, 12, 18, 24 and 30, η = 3 and chunks of 4,096 tokens.
